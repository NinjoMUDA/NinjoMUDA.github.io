Design highlights (4)
  • Why the player is a godAuto-battlers can feel like you're just watching. I wanted a reason for that, so I made the player a god. Gods don't swing swords. They pick the team, hand out blessings and decide where the party goes next, and that's exactly what you do in the game.
  • Testing before tuningWhen a weapon feels off, I don't just guess. I run the same team fight a few times, write down what happened, and only then change something. Doing it this way showed me that two ranged weapons were doing almost the same job, and that one melee weapon was far ahead of the rest, so that's next on my list.
  • Three tries at combatI first tried Tug-of-War lanes, then a grid like TFT. Neither felt right, so I scrapped both. Now units move freely and clash in the middle, but where you place them still matters: your front line keeps enemies off the back row.
  • What happens after a fightYou get a reward screen: gold from normal fights, one piece of loot from a rival party, or a pick of 1 out of 3 artifacts from a boss. Then you choose a skill, see what your heroes gained from leveling up, and head back to the map.

Dungeon Drift

In developmentRoguelike × auto-battler Unity 6 · HD-2DJun 2026 – now

You play a god who isn't at full power yet. You send a human party into the dungeon, arrange them before each fight, and then they battle on their own. Faith you bring back makes your god stronger for the next run.

WhyI wanted a game about drafting a party and building characters that click together in team fights. Auto-chess games do this, but they're competitive matches where your power comes from a random shop each round. I wanted the same team-building in a single-player roguelike, where your build grows across a whole run. No game did that the way I wanted, so I started making one.
Inspired byDota 2 custom auto-battlers (the fights), Slay the Spire (the node map and reward picks), Path of Exile 2 (Ascendancy and item depth)
My roleI lead the design and balance: the world pillars, more than 50 numbered design decisions, and every tuning pass. The code is written with AI coding agents (Claude, Codex) that I direct and review. Most of the art is by my teammate Arth.
StatusIn development. I'm still reworking systems because the core mechanic doesn't feel good enough yet, and getting it right comes before adding content.
All art by my teammates. My part was the core loop and mechanics.

Unknown To Depth

Concept & design docRoguelite · time loop depa Game Accelerator entryTeam Game4Life (5)2023

A cursed hero respawns in the Depth every time he dies, and each death reshapes the floor through a butterfly effect. To break the curse he has to climb a tower of 10 worlds.

Inspired byHades: dying is part of the loop, and every run makes you stronger.
My roleI designed the core game loop and the mechanics: the two-currency economy, the room system and meta-progression.
ResultWe entered depa Game Accelerator with a 37-slide pitch and didn't make the shortlist.
What I learnedWe thought too big: 10 floors, a deep lore and lots of features, while the most important part, the moment-to-moment gameplay, got the least explanation even though it needed the most. Now I start small, nail the core loop first, and design systems that feed into each other instead of piling them up.
Design highlights (3)
  • Two kinds of moneyWe split the money in two. Coins are for the run you're in, so you spend them in shops on items that help right now. Souls carry over between runs, so you save them for permanent upgrades and for hiring troops. Artifacts, cores and runes sit on top of that for stat boosts, weapon evolution and perks.
  • Doors that tell you what's behind themEvery door has a symbol on it: shop, perk, soul room, casino, puzzle or chest. I didn't want picking a path to be a blind guess. When you can see what's behind each door, you're actually weighing risk against reward.
  • Progress that carries overIf you clear a perk room, it stays unlocked from the first floor on your next run, and you get more perk slots over time (up to 4). Dying still costs you the run, but it never feels like you lost everything.
